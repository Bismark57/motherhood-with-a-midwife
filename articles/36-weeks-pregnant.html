36 Weeks Pregnant: What to Expect + Early Signs of Labor

The short answer: At 36 weeks you are about one month from your due date, and pregnancy shifts into the final stretch. In this video Sandra walks through what to expect now: your baby's size, weight and development — especially the lungs and brain — whether your baby is head-down or breech, what baby dropping (lightening) feels like, why your checkups become weekly, the Group B Strep (GBS) swab test, how to tell Braxton Hicks from real contractions with the 5-1-1 rule, the quiet signs labor is getting close, the red flags to never ignore, and a practical 36-week prep checklist.

Upload date: November 3, 2026

Your baby at 36 weeks: size, weight, lungs and brain

Your baby is now roughly the size of a large head of romaine lettuce — around 47 cm long and about 2.6 kg (5¾ lb), and still gaining steadily in these final weeks. Two systems are doing especially important finishing work. The lungs are maturing and producing the surfactant that will let your baby breathe air after birth, and the brain is growing and folding rapidly — growth that continues right up to the birth and beyond. This is why these last weeks matter: a baby born at 36 weeks will usually do well, but every extra week inside is more finishing time for the lungs and brain.

Head-down vs breech: your baby's position

By 36 weeks, most babies have settled head-down in the pelvis — the position that makes a vaginal birth most straightforward. A smaller number are still breech, with their bottom or feet pointing down. Your care team checks position at your appointments now, partly by feeling your bump and partly, if there is any doubt, by scan. If your baby is breech at 36 weeks, there is still a chance of turning, and your team will talk you through the options rather than leaving it to the birth day to find out.

Baby dropping (lightening): breathing relief, bladder pressure

Some time in these final weeks — often around now for a first baby — your baby may drop lower into your pelvis, a shift called lightening. The trade is unmistakable: as the baby moves down, the pressure under your ribs eases and you can suddenly breathe and eat more comfortably — while the pressure on your bladder increases, bringing more pelvic heaviness and more trips to the bathroom. Dropping is a sign your body and baby are preparing, but it is not a countdown clock: labor can still be days or a few weeks away.

Weekly checkups begin — and the Group B Strep swab

From around 36 weeks, prenatal appointments usually switch from monthly or fortnightly to weekly. The visits get shorter and more focused: blood pressure, urine, your baby's position and heartbeat, and how you are feeling as birth approaches. This is also when most women have the Group B Strep (GBS) swab — a quick, simple swab of the vagina and rectum, usually done between 35 and 37 weeks. Group B Strep is a common bacterium that is harmless to you but can, rarely, make a newborn seriously ill if passed on during birth. If your swab is positive, the fix is straightforward: antibiotics through a drip during labor. The test itself takes seconds and most women find it no worse than a smear test.

Braxton Hicks vs real contractions: the 5-1-1 rule

Practice contractions — Braxton Hicks — often become more noticeable at 36 weeks, and the question every mother asks is how to tell them from the real thing. Braxton Hicks are usually irregular, do not settle into a pattern, and tend to fade if you rest, change position, or drink some water. Real labor contractions build: they become regular, closer together, and stronger, and they keep going whatever you do. The video's yardstick is the 5-1-1 rule: when contractions are 5 minutes apart, each lasting 1 minute, for 1 hour, it is time to call and go in. Common 36-week symptoms: sleep, mucus plug and nesting

Sleep often gets harder now — a big bump, a busy bladder, and a mind that will not switch off. Rest whenever you can rather than saving it all for the night. You may also notice losing your mucus plug, a jelly-like discharge that has sealed your cervix through pregnancy; losing it means your cervix is starting to change, not that labor is today. And many women feel a sudden burst of nesting energy — an urge to clean, organise and prepare. Enjoy it in moderation: channel it into the checklist below, not into moving furniture.

5 quiet signs labor is actually getting close

Beyond the dramatic signs, the video points to quieter changes that labor is drawing near: your baby dropping into the pelvis, losing the mucus plug, practice contractions that feel more frequent and more patterned than before, a nesting surge, and your body generally shifting gears — appetite changes, looser bowels, an unsettled, expectant feeling. None of these gives you a date. Together, they mean the final stretch has genuinely begun, and it is time to have everything ready rather than nearly ready.

Red flags: when to call your provider immediately

Most of late pregnancy is uncomfortable rather than dangerous — but some symptoms at 36 weeks should never wait for the next weekly appointment. Call your care team or go in straight away if you notice:

  • Reduced baby movements — your baby's pattern slows or stops. Never wait until morning.
  • Vaginal bleeding beyond light spotting or a bloody show.
  • Your waters breaking — a gush or a steady trickle you cannot stop, especially before 37 weeks.
  • A severe headache, vision changes, or sudden swelling — possible preeclampsia signs, which often appear in these final weeks.
  • Regular, painful contractions before 37 weeks — possible preterm labor.
  • Anything that frightens you. Your instinct counts; maternity teams would always rather check you.

Your 36-week checklist

The video closes with the practical list — finish these this week and you can spend the rest of the final stretch resting:

  • Hospital bag: fully packed and sitting by the front door.
  • Car seat: installed and checked before the baby arrives, not learned in the hospital car park.
  • Birth preferences: talked through with your doctor or midwife at a weekly checkup.
  • Freezer meals: a few easy meals stocked for the first days home.
  • Rest: prioritised on purpose — while your baby finishes the brain and lung growth that these weeks are for.

Common questions

Is 36 weeks full term?

Not quite. A baby born at 36 weeks is "late preterm"; early term begins at 37 weeks and full term at 39. Most babies born at 36 weeks do well, but the lungs and brain are still finishing important growth, which is why these last weeks inside matter and why labor before 37 weeks is a call-your-provider sign.

What is the Group B Strep test, and does it hurt?

It is a quick swab of the vagina and rectum, usually taken between 35 and 37 weeks, checking for a common bacterium that is harmless to you but can rarely infect a newborn during birth. It takes seconds and is no more uncomfortable than a smear test. If it is positive, you simply get antibiotics during labor to protect your baby.

My baby has dropped. Does that mean labor is starting?

It means your baby has settled lower into your pelvis to get ready — you will breathe more easily and feel more bladder pressure. For a first baby, dropping often happens a few weeks before labor; for later babies it may not happen until labor itself. Treat it as "get ready," not "go now."

How do I know if these contractions are real?

Time them. Braxton Hicks are irregular and fade with rest, movement, or a drink. Real contractions build a pattern — regular, closer together, stronger, and continuing whatever you do. When they reach 5 minutes apart, lasting 1 minute, for 1 hour (the 5-1-1 rule), call and go in.
