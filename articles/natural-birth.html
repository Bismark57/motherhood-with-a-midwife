Birth

Natural Birth: Before, During & After

The short answer: A natural birth unfolds in three stages: labour (your cervix opening to 10 cm), the birth itself (pushing your baby out), and delivering the placenta. Beforehand, preparation is mostly mental and physical rehearsal — birth education, breathing and positions, a support person who knows the plan, and a body kept gently active. During labour, most women cope best with movement, water, low light, and continuous support. Afterwards comes the golden hour: skin-to-skin, first feeding, and checks for you and your baby.

Before: preparation that actually matters

You cannot train for birth like a race — but you can arrive informed, and informed women labour with less fear. Learn the stages so sensations have explanations. Practise slow breathing and the positions you'll use (upright, kneeling, on all fours — gravity is your oldest midwifery tool). Choose your support people carefully: calm, steady, and briefed. And prepare your body with ordinary activity — walking, stretching, pelvic floor work, and perineal massage from around 34 weeks if your provider agrees. During: the three stages

  • Early labour: irregular contractions, excitement, nerves. Rest, eat lightly, stay home while it's comfortable — this phase can take hours, especially first time.
  • Active labour: contractions grow longer, stronger, closer (the classic sign: lasting a minute, every 4–5 minutes, for an hour). Movement, the shower or bath, counter-pressure on your back, and your support person's voice carry you through.
  • Transition: the shortest, hardest stretch — shaking, doubt, 'I can't do this' — right before full dilation. Midwives hear that sentence and know the baby is close.
  • Second stage: the urge to push. Follow your body's pushes; your team will guide the baby out gently to protect your perineum.
  • Third stage: the placenta delivers, usually within 30 minutes, with monitoring of your bleeding and a fundal check.

After: the golden hour

Your baby goes straight onto your chest, skin to skin, warm and unhurried. The cord is usually left to finish pulsing before clamping. In that first hour your baby will likely find the breast, you'll be checked for bleeding and any tears needing stitches, and the world narrows to one small face. Whatever your birth looked like — this hour is protected time, and you can ask for it in any birth plan.

Common questions

How painful is natural birth, honestly?

It is intense — most women describe powerful pressure and stretching rather than injury-type pain, and it builds gradually so you adapt. Coping tools, water, movement, and support make a real difference, and choosing pain relief at any point is not a failure of 'natural' birth.

How long does labour take the first time?

On average, active labour in a first birth runs 8–12 hours, with wide normal variation either way. Early labour can add many more hours — pacing yourself early is a genuine strategy.

Can I eat and drink in labour?

Policies vary by hospital and your situation; many now allow light snacks and fluids in low-risk labour. Ask your team what applies to you, and pack honey, juice, or snacks just in case.
