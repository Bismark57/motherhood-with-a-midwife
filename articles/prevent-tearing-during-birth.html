Tearing During Birth: 7 Ways to Actually Prevent It

The short answer: Perineal tearing is one of the most common fears in pregnancy — but there are practical steps that protect your perineum and significantly lower your risk of a severe tear. In this video Sandra walks through the 7 evidence-based ways: perineal massage at 35 weeks, warm compresses during pushing, optimal birthing positions, breathing instead of purple pushing, perineal support as your baby crowns, pelvic floor relaxation and training, and the truth about routine episiotomies. Some start weeks before birth; others are simple things you can ask your provider for on the day.

Upload date: October 28, 2026

Why tearing happens

As your baby's head crowns, the skin and muscle between the vagina and the back passage — the perineum — has to stretch further and faster than it has ever stretched before. If the tissue cannot stretch quickly enough, it tears. First-time mothers tear most often because that tissue has never done this before, and a fast, forceful crowning gives it the least time to adapt. Tears are graded by depth: a first-degree tear is skin only, a second-degree tear reaches the muscle, and third- and fourth-degree tears extend further and need specialist repair. Most tears are first- or second-degree, heal well, and the goal of everything below is simple: give the tissue time, elasticity, and support so that if it stretches, it stretches instead of tearing — and any tear that does happen stays small.

Way 1: Perineal massage at 35 weeks

The best-proven preparation technique in the video starts before labour: from about 35 weeks, gently massaging and stretching the perineum for a few minutes several times a week trains the tissue to stretch and teaches you what that stretching sensation feels like, so it surprises you less on the day. Use clean hands, short nails, and a little natural oil or lubricant; press gently down and outward in a slow U-shape until you feel a mild stretch — never pain.

Way 2: Warm compresses during pushing

Simple and powerful: a cloth soaked in warm water, held against your perineum while you push and as the baby's head crowns. The warmth increases blood flow, softens and relaxes the tissue, and eases the stretching sensation — and warm compresses in the second stage are linked in research with fewer severe tears. Ask your midwife or birth team about it ahead of time so it is part of the plan, not an afterthought.

Way 3: Optimal birthing positions — why side-lying helps

The position you give birth in changes the pressure on your perineum. Lying flat on your back concentrates pressure there. Side-lying in particular takes pressure off the perineum and slows the birth of the head, and hands-and-knees, kneeling, and upright positions also open the pelvis and give you more control as the head is born. You may not finally choose the position until you are in labour — what matters is knowing you are allowed to move, and saying so in your birth preferences.

Way 4: Breathing — not "purple pushing"

"Purple pushing" is the held-breath, face-reddening, counted-to-ten pushing many women are coached into — and it drives the head out fast, which is exactly when tears happen. The alternative in this video: breathe your baby out. Short, soft breaths and open, gentle pushes let the uterus ease the head out millimetre by millimetre while the tissue stretches gradually. When the head is crowning, your midwife may ask you to pant or breathe gently instead of pushing — it takes enormous self-control in the most intense minute of your life, which is why it is worth practising the breathing before the day.

Way 5: Perineal support during crowning

A skilled midwife's hands do two jobs as your baby is born: supporting the perineum so it stretches evenly instead of splitting at its weakest point, and controlling the speed of the head so it is born slowly. This is one reason continuous, attentive support in the second stage matters so much. Tell your team in your birth preferences that you would like hands-on perineal support and a slow, guided crowning — a good team will be glad you asked.

Way 6: Pelvic floor relaxation and training

Most women only ever practise squeezing the pelvic floor. For birth, the skill that matters just as much is the opposite: letting it go. A pelvic floor that stays gripped and tense as the head descends resists the stretch and tears more easily; one that can release lets the baby through. Training in pregnancy means practising both — strengthen, and consciously soften: inhale, and as you exhale, drop and relax the pelvic floor, like the moment you finally sit down after holding on for a long time. Being able to relax on purpose is a real, trainable birth skill.

Way 7: The truth about routine episiotomies

The last "way" is a myth to retire: an episiotomy — a deliberate cut made by your provider — does not protect you when it is done routinely. A cut is a wound of its own, it can extend into a deeper tear, and natural tissue generally heals better than a cut of the same depth. Episiotomy still has a genuine place when a baby needs to be born quickly or instruments are needed — but as an emergency tool, not a routine preventive measure, and your team should explain before doing one whenever time allows. Knowing this lets you ask the right question in your birth preferences: no routine episiotomy, please.

If you do tear

Even doing all seven, some women still tear — a fast labour, a large baby, or a baby in an awkward position can outweigh the best preparation, and it is never the mother's fault. Most tears are repaired shortly after birth with stitches that dissolve on their own. Keep the area clean, take the pain relief you are offered, and tell your care team promptly about increasing pain, swelling, an odour, or a feeling that a stitch has opened — those are the signs that need a check, not patience.

Common questions

Can I guarantee I won't tear?

No honest guide can promise that. What these seven ways do is lower your chances and, just as importantly, reduce how severe a tear is if one happens — and severity is what makes the biggest difference to recovery.

When should I start perineal massage?

Around 35 weeks, for a few minutes several times a week, is the advice in this video. Check with your own provider first if you have a vaginal infection, bleeding, or a placenta covering the cervix — those change the advice.

Is an episiotomy better than tearing?

That is Way 7 in the video: a deliberate cut does not protect you when done routinely, and natural tissue generally heals better than a cut of the same depth. It still has a place when the baby needs to be born quickly, and your team should explain before doing one whenever time allows.

Does this apply if it isn't my first baby?

Yes. Tearing is less common after a previous vaginal birth because the tissue has stretched before, but the in-the-room ways — position, warm compress, slow breathing out, and hands-on support — protect every mother, every time.
