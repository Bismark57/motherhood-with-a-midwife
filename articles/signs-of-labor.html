Signs of Labor: When to Actually Go to the Hospital

The short answer: It is 3 a.m., you are 39 weeks pregnant, and something feels different — is this it? In this video Sandra sorts every sign of labor honestly into three buckets: labor is coming soon (days away), labor has really started, and go to the hospital now. That includes the famous sign everyone gets wrong, the quiet sign that means go even with no contractions, waters breaking explained — usually a trickle, not a flood — the 5-1-1 rule, why the timing rules completely change for a second baby, what to do at home in early labor, and what actually happens when you arrive at the hospital.

Upload date: October 29, 2026

Bucket 1: Signs labor is coming soon

The first bucket is good news, not an emergency: your body is preparing, and labor is likely days — sometimes longer — away. The video walks through six "coming soon" signs, including your baby dropping lower into your pelvis (you may breathe a little easier and feel more pelvic pressure), losing your mucus plug, and a bloody show — blood-tinged mucus as your cervix begins to change. None of these, on their own, means you need to leave for the hospital. They mean the countdown has started, your bag should be ready, and you should rest while you can.

The #1 sign most parents get wrong

One famous sign in particular sends almost everyone into a panic — and Sandra singles it out in this video because it is so often misunderstood. A single dramatic-looking sign, on its own and without a contraction pattern behind it, does not mean labor has started today. What turns a "coming soon" sign into real labor is never one moment — it is the pattern that builds behind it. Watch this part of the video before your due date, so that when it happens to you at 3 a.m. you can recognise it calmly instead of rushing.

Bucket 2: True labor has started — real contractions vs prodromal labor

The second bucket is about contractions — and telling real contractions from prodromal labor, the frustrating stop-start contractions that can come and go for days without your cervix progressing. Sandra's answer is a 4-point pattern check: time your contractions and look at the pattern, not a single pain. Are they becoming regular? Are they coming closer together? Are they getting stronger? And do they keep going — rather than fading when you rest, move, or drink? A pattern that builds in all those ways is true labor starting. A pattern that stalls, spaces out, or stops is prodromal — exhausting and completely real-feeling, but not yet the day.

When your waters break: trickle vs gush, and the colour check

Forget the movie version. For most women, waters breaking is a trickle, not a flood — a slow leak you might first mistake for something else — though a gush is possible too. What matters most is the colour check: put on a pad and look. Clear fluid is the reassuring picture. If the fluid is green or brown, that is the meconium rule — your baby has passed their first stool into the waters, and that means go and be checked, even if you feel well. Note the time your waters broke, the colour, and whether contractions have started, and tell your care team all three.

Bucket 3: When to go to the hospital NOW

The third bucket needs no weighing up — if any of these are true, go now, or call your maternity unit straight away:

  • The 5-1-1 rule: contractions are 5 minutes apart, each lasting 1 minute, and that pattern has held for 1 hour.
  • Your waters have broken — especially if the fluid is green or brown, or if contractions have not started and your team wants you assessed.
  • A quiet baby: your baby's movements have slowed or stopped. This is the quiet sign in the video that means go even with no contractions at all — never wait until morning to have it checked.
  • Bleeding that is more than a bloody show.
  • Your gut: something feels wrong in a way you cannot name. Sandra is clear — your instinct counts as a sign, and maternity teams would rather check you ten times than miss the one time it mattered.

Second or third baby? Why your timeline changes

If this is not your first baby, do not reuse your first baby's timing rules. Labors after the first are often significantly faster — your body has done this before, and the second stage in particular can move quickly. That means calling earlier, leaving earlier, and not waiting for a textbook 5-1-1 pattern at home while things accelerate. If your first labor was fast, tell your maternity team in advance and agree a plan for when to come in.

What to do during early labor at home

Early labor is usually the longest part — and for most women, the best place for it is home. Rest while you can, even if you only doze between contractions. Eat lightly and keep drinking. Time contractions in bursts to check the pattern, rather than watching the clock all night. Move, change positions, use warmth or a shower if they help, and keep your birth partner close. You are not "doing nothing" at home — you are saving your energy and your calm for the part where you will need them most, and arriving too early often just means a longer, more tiring hospital stay.

Arriving at the hospital: what actually happens in triage

When you arrive, you will usually be assessed first in triage before anyone decides about admitting you. Expect your contractions and your baby's heartbeat to be monitored, your blood pressure and temperature taken, questions about your waters — when they broke and what colour the fluid was — and, with your consent, possibly a vaginal examination to check dilation. If you are in established labor, you will be admitted to a birth room. If it is still very early, the team may honestly suggest going home again for a while — that is not rejection, it is the pattern check working in your favour.

Trusting your instincts — and when to call

The video ends where every good birth plan should: you do not need to diagnose yourself perfectly before you are allowed to call. If your waters break, if your baby goes quiet, if you are bleeding, if a contraction pattern is building, or if your gut simply says something is different — call. That is what the maternity line is for, at 3 a.m. as much as at 3 p.m.

Common questions

How do I know if it is real labor or prodromal labor?

Use the video's 4-point pattern check: time your contractions and look for a pattern that is regular, coming closer together, getting stronger, and keeping going despite rest or movement. That building pattern is true labor. Contractions that fade, space out, or stop are prodromal labor — real-feeling, but not yet progressing.

What is the 5-1-1 rule?

Go to the hospital when your contractions are 5 minutes apart, each lasts 1 minute, and that pattern has continued for 1 hour. If this is your second or later baby, call earlier — your timeline changes, and waiting for the full 5-1-1 pattern can leave it too late.

My waters broke but I have no contractions. What should I do?

Check the colour on a pad, note the time, and call your maternity unit. Clear fluid with a well, moving baby is less urgent than green or brown fluid — the meconium rule — which means go and be checked straight away. Your team will advise on timing because waters breaking starts a clock even before contractions do.

My baby has gone quiet, but I have no contractions. Should I wait?

No. A quiet baby — movements slowed or stopped — is a go-now sign in this video even with no contractions at all. Contact your maternity unit or go in right away, day or night. Never wait until morning.
