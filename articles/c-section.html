Birth

What Happens During a C-Section? Step by Step

The short answer: A caesarean section is an operation to birth your baby through an incision in your abdomen and womb. With a spinal anaesthetic you're awake but feel no pain. From the first incision to meeting your baby usually takes about 5–10 minutes, and the whole operation about 45–60 minutes. The video below shows every step in gentle 3D detail.

Before the operation

Whether your C-section is planned or decided during labour, the preparation is similar. You'll have a drip (IV) in your hand, a catheter to empty your bladder, and blood tests. Most caesareans use a spinal or epidural anaesthetic — an injection in your back that numbs you from the chest down. You stay awake, your birth partner can sit beside you, and you get to hear your baby's first cry. A screen is placed across your chest so you don't see the operation itself.

The steps, one by one

  • The incision. Once the anaesthetic is working (checked carefully), the surgeon makes a horizontal cut, usually low across the bikini line, through the skin and layers of the abdomen.
  • Reaching the womb. The abdominal muscles are gently parted — not cut — and a second incision is made in the lower part of the womb.
  • Your baby is born. The surgeon guides your baby out through the opening. You may feel pressure or a tugging sensation, but no pain. This is the moment the whole room has been working toward — your baby is lifted up so you can see them.
  • Cord and placenta. The cord is clamped and cut, and the placenta is delivered through the same opening.
  • Closing. The womb and abdomen are closed in layers with dissolving stitches, and the skin with stitches, staples, or glue. This part takes the longest — most of the 45–60 minutes.

Straight after

You'll spend an hour or two in recovery while the numbness wears off, usually with your baby skin-to-skin as soon as you're both ready. Most mothers are up and walking within a day, and home within 2–4 days. Full recovery takes about six weeks — this was major surgery, and your body deserves the same patience you'd give any operation.

A midwife's reassurance

Nearly one in three babies in many countries now arrives this way. A C-section is not a "failed" birth — it is a birth, and often the safest door into the world for a particular mother and baby. However your baby arrives, you did that.

Common questions

Will I feel pain during a C-section?

You should feel no pain. The team tests the block thoroughly before starting — you'll feel touch, pressure, and movement, but not sharpness or pain. If you feel anything painful, say so immediately; there is always more that can be done, including general anaesthetic if needed.

Can my partner be with me?

In most hospitals, yes — one support person can sit by your head throughout a spinal/epidural caesarean. For a general anaesthetic (where you're asleep), partners usually wait nearby and meet the baby as soon as possible.

Can I have a vaginal birth next time?

Often, yes. Many women have a successful vaginal birth after caesarean (VBAC), depending on the reason for the first C-section and the type of incision on the womb. It's a conversation to have with your own care team in your next pregnancy.
