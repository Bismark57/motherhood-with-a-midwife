Stay Safe

9 Foods to Avoid in Pregnancy — and What's Actually Safe

The short answer: Almost everything is still on the table in pregnancy. A short list of foods carries real risks — mostly listeria, salmonella, and mercury — because pregnancy softens your immune system and a few things can reach your baby. The nine foods in this guide are the ones to avoid, limit, or simply prepare differently — and every single one has an easy safe swap, so this is about eating with knowledge, not fear.

Why food safety matters more in pregnancy

In pregnancy your immune system deliberately dials itself down so it doesn't reject your baby — and that truce makes food-borne infections more likely, and more serious. Listeria, a bacteria that can grow even in the fridge, can cross the placenta. Salmonella and toxoplasma can make you very ill at a time when your body is working for two. And mercury in large fish builds up and can affect your baby's developing brain. That is the whole logic of this list: nine foods, a handful of real risks — and simple swaps for each. In this video I count them down from 9 to 1, ending with deli meat at number one — the one almost every mother is eating.

The 9 foods, in brief

  • 9 · Raw eggs — can carry salmonella. Watch the hidden places: homemade mayonnaise, salad dressings, mousse, tiramisu, and raw batter. Safe swap: eggs cooked until firm, and pasteurised-egg versions of the creamy things.
  • 8 · Unwashed produce — soil can carry toxoplasma and listeria, and it clings to skins and leaves. Safe swap: nothing here is banned — just wash fruit, vegetables, and salads well under running water.
  • 7 · Raw fish and high-mercury fish — raw seafood can carry bacteria and parasites, and big predator fish like swordfish, shark, and king mackerel collect mercury. Safe swap: cooked fish, and low-mercury choices like salmon a couple of times a week.
  • 6 · Pâté and liver — pâté can carry listeria, and liver packs very high levels of vitamin A (retinol), which in excess can harm your baby's development. Safe swap: other spreads and proteins; the vitamin A in vegetables (beta-carotene) is safe and plenty.
  • 5 · Herbal teas — herbs are medicines in a cup, and most haven't been properly tested in pregnancy; some can stimulate the uterus or interact with medication. Safe swap: check the specific tea with your care provider before making it a daily habit — medicinal blends and supplements are the ones to check first.
  • 4 · Soft cheeses — soft, mould-ripened, and unpasteurised cheeses (brie, camembert, some feta and blue cheeses) can carry listeria. Safe swap: pasteurised versions, hard cheeses, or soft cheese cooked until steaming.
  • 3 · Caffeine — not banned; the limit is the point. Keep your total to about 200 mg a day from all sources — coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks, and chocolate all count. Safe swap: decaf, or one real coffee enjoyed properly instead of three on autopilot.
  • 2 · Raw sprouts — alfalfa, clover, and bean sprouts grow in warm, damp conditions that bacteria love, and washing can't reliably remove them. Safe swap: sprouts cooked through — in stir-fries and soups they belong.
  • 1 · Deli meat — the surprise at number one: listeria can grow slowly even in the fridge, so cold deli meats and hot dogs carry this list's most everyday risk. Safe swap: heat deli meat until it's steaming hot — then enjoy it without a second thought.

Already eaten something on this list?

Then hear this first: the risk from any single serving is small, and listeriosis is rare — most mothers who ate the 'wrong' thing have perfectly healthy babies. Don't spiral. The symptoms worth knowing are a fever and flu-like aching in the days after; if that happens, call your care provider and mention what you ate. Otherwise, let it go, and use the swaps from here on.

Common questions

Can I eat sushi at all while pregnant?

Yes — cooked sushi, tempura rolls, and vegetable rolls are all fine. It's the raw fish you're skipping, not the restaurant. If the fish is cooked through, you're within the guidance.

Is all cheese off the table?

Not at all. Hard cheeses like cheddar are fine, and pasteurised soft cheeses are fine too. The caution is unpasteurised and mould-ripened soft cheese — and even those are safe cooked until steaming, which is baked camembert's famous loophole.

How much caffeine is really okay?

Around 200 mg a day is the widely used limit — roughly one large mug of filter coffee, or two smaller instant coffees. Count tea, cola, and chocolate too, and be wary of energy drinks, which stack caffeine fast.
