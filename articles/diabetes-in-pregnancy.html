Stay Safe

Diabetes in Pregnancy: Everything You Need to Know

The short answer: Diabetes in pregnancy usually means gestational diabetes — blood sugar that rises in pregnancy because placental hormones make your body resist insulin. It's typically picked up by a glucose screening test at 24–28 weeks, causes no symptoms in most women, and is managed first with food, movement, and home blood-sugar checks; some women also need metformin or insulin. Well-managed, most mothers and babies do beautifully — unmanaged, high sugars grow a bigger baby and complicate birth, which is why the screening matters.

Why pregnancy stresses blood sugar

From mid-pregnancy, hormones from the placenta deliberately make your cells resist insulin — a design that shunts glucose to your growing baby. Most mothers simply make more insulin and nothing changes. In gestational diabetes, the pancreas can't quite keep up, and blood sugar creeps above the safe range. Risk is higher with a family history of diabetes, PCOS, a previous big baby or gestational diabetes, and higher starting weight — but it can appear in women with none of these, which is why screening is offered to everyone. Managing it day to day

  • Testing: you'll be taught to check your blood sugar at home — typically fasting and after meals — and to log the numbers for your team.
  • Food: no special 'diabetic diet' — rather, carbohydrates spread across the day, paired with protein and fibre, and sugary drinks off the table. A dietitian referral is standard and genuinely helpful.
  • Movement: a 10–15 minute walk after meals is one of the most effective sugar-lowering tools there is.
  • Medication: if food and activity aren't enough, metformin and/or insulin are safe, well-studied next steps — needing them is not a failure.
  • Extra monitoring: growth scans in the third trimester, because sugar feeds growth.

Pre-existing diabetes? Different lane, earlier start

If you already have type 1 or type 2 diabetes, pregnancy planning ideally starts before conception — tight sugar control in the first weeks protects your baby's developing organs, and you'll need specialist care, folic acid at the prescribed higher dose, and early eye and kidney checks. Tell your diabetes team the moment pregnancy is on the horizon.

Birth and afterwards

With gestational diabetes, birth is often planned a little earlier than 40 weeks if sugars or growth suggest it, and your baby's blood sugar is checked in the first hours (early feeding helps). For most mothers, blood sugar returns to normal after birth — but the diagnosis is a lifelong memo: your risk of type 2 diabetes is higher, so the follow-up glucose test at 6 weeks to 6 months postpartum, and regular checks after, genuinely matter. Many women use the diagnosis as the push that protects their next 30 years of health.

Common questions

Does gestational diabetes mean my baby will have diabetes?

No. Your baby is not born with diabetes. Babies of mothers with high sugars can grow larger and may have low blood sugar briefly after birth (they're used to producing extra insulin) — which is why early feeding and those first checks happen.

Will I need a caesarean?

Not automatically. Many women with well-managed gestational diabetes have straightforward vaginal births. The plan depends on your baby's growth, your sugars, and your own history — a conversation with your team, not a foregone conclusion.

Can I prevent gestational diabetes?

Not entirely — genes and placenta have their say. A healthy weight before pregnancy, regular activity, and balanced eating reduce the risk, but women who do everything 'right' still get it. It is a hormone condition, not a character verdict.
