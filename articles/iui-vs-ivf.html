IUI vs IVF: Which Is Better for Getting Pregnant?

The short answer: Neither treatment is simply "better" — they suit different situations. IUI (intrauterine insemination) is generally less invasive and less expensive per cycle: prepared sperm are placed directly into the uterus around ovulation, and fertilisation still happens inside the body. IVF (in vitro fertilisation) involves more procedures and usually a higher upfront cost, but eggs are collected and fertilised in the laboratory, which makes it the option that can work when IUI cannot — for example when the fallopian tubes are blocked. Neither treatment guarantees pregnancy. The most suitable option depends on your age, the cause of infertility, fallopian tube health, sperm factors, and any previous treatment.

What IUI and IVF actually are

The names sound similar, but where fertilisation happens is completely different. With IUI, the laboratory prepares a concentrated sample of sperm, and a clinician places it through the cervix into the uterus close to ovulation — the sperm still have to swim to the egg and fertilise it in the fallopian tube, as in natural conception. With IVF, the ovaries are stimulated to produce several eggs, the eggs are collected, and fertilisation happens in the laboratory. An embryo is then transferred into the uterus a few days later. In this video Sandra walks through both processes honestly, step by step, so you know what each one actually involves before you are sitting in a clinic hearing the words for the first time.

How IUI works, step by step

  • Monitoring: your cycle is tracked — sometimes with fertility medication to encourage one or two eggs to mature, sometimes in a natural cycle.
  • Timing: insemination is scheduled around ovulation, when the egg is released.
  • Preparation: the sperm sample is washed and concentrated in the laboratory, selecting the most active sperm.
  • Insemination: a thin catheter passes through the cervix and the prepared sperm are placed into the uterus — a brief procedure, usually with little or no pain for most women.
  • The wait: fertilisation, if it happens, takes place in the fallopian tube. A pregnancy test follows about two weeks later.

Because fertilisation happens inside the body, IUI needs at least one open fallopian tube and a sperm sample with enough moving sperm to make the journey worthwhile.

How IVF works, step by step

  • Stimulation: daily hormone injections encourage the ovaries to mature several eggs in one cycle instead of the usual one, with scans and blood tests to monitor progress.
  • Egg collection: when the eggs are ready, they are collected through the vagina with a fine needle, usually under sedation.
  • Fertilisation: in the laboratory, eggs and sperm are brought together — or, in some cases, a single sperm is injected into an egg (ICSI).
  • Embryo culture: fertilised eggs are watched for several days as they develop into embryos.
  • Embryo transfer: one embryo is placed through the cervix into the uterus. A pregnancy test follows about two weeks later. Any remaining suitable embryos may be frozen for future attempts.

Because fertilisation happens in the laboratory and the embryo is placed directly in the uterus, IVF bypasses the fallopian tubes entirely — which is why it can be the appropriate route when tubes are blocked or severely damaged.

The key differences at a glance

  • Where fertilisation happens: inside the body (fallopian tube) with IUI; in the laboratory with IVF.
  • Invasiveness: IUI is a single brief procedure per cycle. IVF involves daily injections, monitoring, an egg-collection procedure, and a transfer.
  • Cost per cycle: IUI costs considerably less per cycle. IVF usually has a much higher upfront cost, and medication adds to both.
  • Chance per cycle: IVF generally offers a higher chance of pregnancy per cycle than IUI — but age and diagnosis change the picture a great deal (see below).
  • What it can overcome: IUI cannot overcome blocked tubes or very low sperm numbers. IVF can address many of those situations, including with ICSI for severe male-factor infertility.

Success rates — and why age matters most

Success rates are the numbers everyone wants, and the numbers most often quoted badly. Three honest points from the video:

  • Age is the single biggest factor for both treatments. The video draws on the CDC's 2021 ART Fertility Clinic and National Summary Report, which reports IVF live-birth rates by age for women using their own eggs — rates fall as age rises, steeply after the late thirties.
  • IUI success per cycle is lower than IVF, and it also depends heavily on age, diagnosis, and sperm quality. A large published analysis of around 10,000 IUI cycles (Stone et al.) is among the sources the video uses to explain who IUI tends to work for — and when repeating it stops making sense.
  • Registry figures are group averages. They describe thousands of other people's outcomes, not your personal chance. Your specialist, who knows your test results, can estimate your individual chances far better than any headline percentage.

The video also explains the reassessment point specialists use: if IUI has not worked after several cycles, the evidence — including the FORT-T trial and ASRM guidance on unexplained infertility — supports discussing whether moving to IVF offers a better path than continuing with more of the same.

Who might consider IUI, and who might consider IVF?

IUI is often considered first when the fallopian tubes are open and the diagnosis is unexplained infertility, mild male-factor infertility, or ovulation problems that respond to medication — including for some women with PCOS whose ovulation can be induced. It is simpler and cheaper, so where the chances are reasonable it is a sensible first step.

IVF may be recommended when the fallopian tubes are blocked or damaged, male-factor infertility is severe, IUI has already been tried without success, age makes time more pressing, or other individual circumstances make the higher per-cycle chance worth the greater cost and effort. Neither list is a rule — they are starting points for a conversation with your specialist about your own results.

Costs, briefly and honestly

Costs vary widely by clinic and country, and medication can change the total significantly, so treat any figure as a starting question rather than an answer. In Ontario, the Ontario Fertility Program funds one IVF cycle for eligible patients at participating clinics — though fertility medications are usually not included and wait lists apply — while IUI procedures may be funded with medication costs separate. Wherever you live, ask a clinic for a full written breakdown: treatment, monitoring, laboratory work, medication, and freezing, so the number you plan around is the real number.

Five questions to ask your fertility specialist

  • Based on my test results, what is causing our difficulty conceiving — and does that point to IUI or IVF?
  • What is my realistic chance per cycle with each option, at my age?
  • How many IUI cycles would you recommend before we reassess?
  • What will each option cost in total, including medication — and is any funding available to me?
  • What are the risks and side effects I should know about, and what happens if the first treatment does not work?

Common questions

Can you try IUI before IVF?

Often, yes — where the tubes are open and the diagnosis and age make the chances reasonable, IUI is commonly tried first because it is simpler and less expensive. But not always: blocked tubes, severe male-factor infertility, or age can make going straight to IVF the better advice. That is a decision to make with your specialist, based on your results.

Does IVF guarantee pregnancy?

No. An embryo transfer is not the same as implantation, and implantation is not the same as a live birth. IVF generally offers the highest chance per cycle of the available treatments, but no treatment can guarantee pregnancy — anyone who promises otherwise is not being straight with you.

Are IUI and IVF painful?

IUI is usually a brief procedure that most women describe as similar to a smear test. IVF involves daily injections, blood tests and scans, and an egg-collection procedure usually done under sedation; the embryo transfer itself is typically quick and similar in feel to IUI. Sandra describes what each stage actually involves in the video.

When do you take a pregnancy test after treatment?

About two weeks after insemination or embryo transfer — the clinic will give you the exact day. Testing much earlier risks a misleading result, because trigger medication can linger and a very early pregnancy may not yet register.

Why might IUI or IVF fail?

Most often, because the embryo — even a healthy-looking one — does not implant, and age-related egg quality is the largest single factor. A failed cycle is information, not a verdict: it helps your specialist adjust medication, timing, or the treatment itself. The video closes with the questions worth asking after a failed cycle.
