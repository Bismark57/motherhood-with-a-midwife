What Is Midwifery? Its Branches — and What Midwives Do

The short answer: Midwifery is the profession of caring for women and their babies through pregnancy, labour and birth, and the first weeks of a baby's life. A midwife is a specialist in normal pregnancy and birth: she supports the body's own process, watches carefully for complications, and brings in a doctor when care needs to go beyond the normal. The word itself tells the story — "midwife" comes from old English meaning "with woman."

A profession of its own — in most of the world

In Ghana, where I trained and practised, midwifery is a distinct profession with its own training, its own licensing, and its own proud history — and the same is true across much of the world. In Canada, much of this same care is carried out within nursing and medicine, which is why my own path here is nursing studies. Whichever country you're in, the heart of the work is the same: skilled, personal care for a mother and her baby at the most important threshold of their lives.

The branches of midwifery

Most people meet a midwife only on the day of birth — but birth is just one branch of the tree. Here is the whole of it:

1. Antenatal care — the pregnancy months

The longest branch. Check-ups through pregnancy: measuring growth, blood pressure and urine checks, blood tests and screening, listening to the baby, and teaching the mother what is normal, what is not, and how to prepare. Good antenatal care is where dangers like preeclampsia are caught early — while they can still be managed.

2. Intrapartum care — labour and birth

The branch everyone pictures: supporting a woman through labour, monitoring her and the baby, guiding the birth, and recognising the moment a labour stops being normal — and acting immediately when it does.

3. Postnatal care — the mother's recovery

After birth, the midwife watches the mother's healing: bleeding, stitches, blood pressure, infection, and her emotional wellbeing in the vulnerable first weeks — including spotting the signs of postnatal depression and getting her support.

4. Newborn care — the baby's first weeks

The baby's first examinations, cord and eye care, warmth, weight checks — and above all, feeding support. Most "my baby won't settle" worries of the first month land in this branch.

5. Family planning and reproductive health

Counselling on contraception, spacing children, fertility awareness, and care before pregnancy even begins. Healthy motherhood starts before conception.

6. Community and public-health midwifery

Midwives in clinics, villages, and neighbourhoods: health education, home visits, immunisation support, and reaching mothers who would otherwise get no care at all. In Ghana this branch carries entire communities.

7. Specialised practice, education and research

Experienced midwives go deeper: adolescent maternity care, bereavement care for families who lose a baby, supporting high-risk pregnancies alongside doctors — and teaching the next generation of midwives, as well as the research that improves care for everyone.

The thread through every branch

Continuity and watchfulness. The midwife's skill is knowing normal so well that the abnormal stands out instantly — and building enough trust that a mother actually tells her what she's feeling. Technique can be taught. That trust is the profession.

Midwife, nurse, doctor — how do they fit together?

  • The midwife specialises in normal pregnancy, birth, and newborn care — and in detecting when things leave normal.
  • The nurse cares across the whole hospital and community — in maternity units, nurses carry much of the day-to-day care of mothers and babies, especially in Canada.
  • The doctor (obstetrician) handles complications and surgery — the caesarean, the difficult birth, the emergency.

Good maternity care is a team, not a competition. The safest systems are the ones where each professional knows the others' strengths — and hands over without ego the moment a mother needs more than their branch can give.

Common questions

Is a midwife the same as a nurse?

Not everywhere. In Ghana and many other countries, midwifery is its own profession with separate training from nursing. In other places the two are combined — a nurse-midwife — and in Canada much maternity care is provided within nursing and medicine. It depends on the country's system.

Do midwives only attend home births?

No. Midwives work in hospitals, clinics, birth centres, and the community — most births attended by midwives worldwide happen in facilities, not homes.

When does a midwife involve a doctor?

Whenever care leaves the normal path: high blood pressure or preeclampsia, a baby in distress, labour that stops progressing, heavy bleeding, or a baby needing resuscitation. Knowing when to hand over is not a weakness of midwifery — it is one of its core skills.
